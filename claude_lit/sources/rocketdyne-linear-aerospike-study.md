# Rocketdyne — Linear Aerospike Engine Study (NASA CR-135231)

## Identity

H. G. Diem and F. M. Kirby, *Linear Aerospike Engine Study, Final Report* (June 1976 –
April 1977), Rockwell International / Rocketdyne Division, prepared for NASA-Lewis Research
Center under Contract NAS3-20114. Report no. RI/RD77-170, NASA CR-135231, dated November
1977. `literature/NASA CR-135231 - Linear Aerospike Engine Study.pdf` (NTRS 19780003139; 236 PDF leaves, direct page-for-page correspondence with
the printed report — PDF leaf N ≈ printed p.(N-1), e.g. PDF leaf 51 = printed p.42/§heading
"Combustion Chamber/Nozzle Geometry"). Tag: `[Aerospike-CR135231]`.

## Character

A NASA-Lewis-funded parametric/point-design study (not a textbook or design-criteria
monograph) of one specific aerospike engine architecture: the **dual-fuel, modular,
split-combustor linear aerospike engine** for a mixed-mode single-stage-to-orbit (SSTO)
vehicle. "Split combustor" means the engine has two independent combustor rows feeding a
single shared linear-spike expansion surface: an **outer combustor** (burns LOX with a
hydrocarbon fuel — RP-1, RJ-5/Shelldyne, or CH4 — used only during "Mode 1," the low-
altitude/high-thrust phase) and an **inner combustor** (always LOX/LH2, used in both Mode 1
and, alone, in throttled-down "Mode 2" after outer-combustor shutdown for the high-altitude/
orbit-insertion phase). This dual-fuel/dual-mode arrangement is itself a design choice
specific to this study's SSTO propellant-density optimization, not a generic aerospike
requirement — a single-propellant linear aerospike (as later flown on the X-33/Linear
Aerospike SR-71 testbed, not covered by this report) can dispense with the split-combustor,
Mode-1/Mode-2 machinery entirely. The bulk of the report (~170 of 236 pages, Tasks I–V) is
parametric trade-space data (performance vs. area ratio/thrust split/chamber pressure/
number of modules/W-H ratio) generated to feed *other* NASA SSTO vehicle studies, not
intended as narrative technical exposition — figures/tables dominate, with short prose
sections explaining method and summarizing trends. Task VI (p.169–212) is the only
"finished preliminary design" content: two point-design engine configurations, taken to a
real hardware layout (module cross-sections, structural frame, turbopump placement,
start/shutdown sequencing, thrust-vector-control geometry, weight breakdown).

OCR quality is poor throughout (character-garbled, words split/concatenated, subscripts
lost — e.g. "aerosplke," "l02/RP-I," "cA_paln, ig9rgmzst_ ion"); all quotations/paraphrases
below were reconstructed by eye from the raw extracted text and cross-checked against
adjacent context. Several table/figure pages (esp. Appendix B design-parameter tables,
p.216–225) are OCR-garbled beyond reconstruction and were not extracted.

## Key results

### What makes a linear aerospike's contour/flow physics different from a bell (the
### requesting session's core question)

**Contour design method** `[Aerospike-CR135231 p.42, "Combustion Chamber/Nozzle Geometry"]`:
generated by a **Rocketdyne method-of-characteristics program**, in three steps —
(1) design an ideal (isentropic, shock-free) **plane-flow spike** contour at the overall
Mode 2 area ratio, using classic minimum-length-nozzle MOC theory generalized to 2-D
planar (not axisymmetric) flow; (2) truncate the *inner*-combustor shroud contour at a
downstream wall angle of ~6° and translate it along the spike's ordinate until its inner
wall matches the ideal spike — this yields the inner-combustor shroud; (3) generate the
*outer*-combustor shroud the same way, selecting the truncation that gives the outer
shroud the same exit pressure as the inner shroud, then aligning the two shroud exits with
the spike. A single example point design: **114:1 Mode-2 area ratio, 42.8:1 Mode-1 area
ratio** (Fig. 30). This is a genuinely different contour-generation procedure from Sutton's
Rao-bell parabolic-approximation method already cited in `topics/02-nozzle-contour-design.md`
— it is a true MOC (characteristics-net) solve of an ideal 2-D spike, not a curve-fit to a
family of pre-computed bell shapes.

**Altitude compensation mechanism**: stated only in one summary sentence
`[Aerospike-CR135231 p.5, Introduction]`: *"there is the inherent automatic and continuous
altitude compensation feature which is characteristic for aerospike nozzles"* — the report
treats this as a known/established property of the aerospike class, not something it
derives or quantifies itself (no base-pressure-vs-altitude curve or free-jet-boundary
analysis is given anywhere in the extracted material). This is the aerospike's headline
selling point vs. a fixed-geometry bell (which is only shock-free/ideally-expanded at one
design altitude) but this report does not supply first-principles altitude-compensation
physics — it only exploits the *consequence* (a single engine that's efficient from sea
level to vacuum without a two-position nozzle) as a design driver.

**Base-region flow / plug truncation**: the "spike" in this design is a **truncated
two-dimensional spike** `[Aerospike-CR135231 p.20, Task II]` — not a full-length ideal spike
run to a point, but cut off partway down its ideal contour, with the remaining base area
closed by a **base closure** shared between two opposing engine modules (each module
supplies one expansion *surface*; modules always come in even-numbered pairs so the base
closure between them is structurally shared). No base-bleed, base-pressure, or
recirculating-base-flow analysis is given in the extracted sections — the report defines
the base closure only as a structural/geometric feature (a hardware boundary, described
under the module structural layout, p.180 Fig. 117 vicinity), not a flow phenomenon it
models. This is a real gap relative to what a full aerospike treatment would need (base
drag/base heating from the low-pressure recirculating base flow is a classic aerospike
design driver in the wider literature this report does not touch).

**Real performance/geometry numbers, point design** `[Aerospike-CR135231 Table 5, p.23]`:
Combustor Throat Geometry for the baseline (thrust-split 0.65, Mode-1 area ratio 40)
point design — outer-combustor aerodynamic throat area 0.439 m² (680 in²), throat gap
2.11 cm (0.83 in); inner-combustor aerodynamic throat area 0.236 m² (366 in²), throat gap
1.14 cm (0.45 in). "Throat gap" (not throat *diameter* — this is a slot/linear throat,
gap = the narrow dimension, engine *width* is the long dimension) is the linear-aerospike-
specific throat parameter replacing a bell's throat radius.

**Mode-1/Mode-2 area-ratio coupling** `[Aerospike-CR135231 Table 4, p.23]`: because the
spike contour is a single fixed physical surface, Mode-1 (both combustors firing) and
Mode-2 (inner combustor only, after outer shutdown) area ratios are linked, not
independently choosable — e.g. Mode-1 ε₁=40 at thrust split 0.65 forces Mode-2 ε₂=114; at
thrust split 0.5 the same ε₁=40 gives ε₂=80; at 0.8, ε₂=200. This ε₁→ε₂ coupling (driven by
thrust split, i.e. how much of the throat area belongs to the outer vs. inner combustor) is
a structural consequence of a single shared spike surface and has no bell-nozzle analog.

### Cooling — what's genuinely nozzle-geometry-specific vs. generic regen

**Shallow throat curvature**: the linear aerospike throat region is explicitly described as
having **shallow curvature** relative to a conventional bell/conical throat
`[Aerospike-CR135231 p.51, coolant-side correlation discussion]` — the RP-1 coolant
correlation's curvature-enhancement factor φ_c is set to **1.0** (no enhancement) specifically
*because of* this shallow curvature, versus the boost a tightly-curved bell throat would give
regen heat transfer. This is a real, quantified consequence of the linear/2-D throat
geometry (a slot has much larger radius of curvature in the flow direction than an
axisymmetric throat of comparable gap) — worth flagging if `engine_designer/` ever adds a
linear/slot-throat option, since the existing Bartz/curvature-enhancement treatment in
`physics/cooling.py` implicitly assumes axisymmetric throat curvature.

**Split circuit topology, chosen for lowest ΔP** `[Aerospike-CR135231 p.42-43, "Thrust
Chamber Cooling Circuits"]`: four candidate cooling-circuit topologies were compared; the
selected one ("Circuit No. 2") cools each combustor (outer, inner) in its own parallel
up-pass circuit and the **nozzle separately, in a single down-pass circuit downstream of
the combustors** — chosen specifically because putting combustor and nozzle circuits in
*parallel* (rather than series) minimizes total coolant ΔP, and because it lets the nozzle
be cooled with a *different* coolant than either combustor if desired. Materials split by
zone: high-heat-flux zirconium-copper (Zr-Cu) channel-wall construction for the
combustors/throat region (chosen for "fin conduction" through the channel land, superior to
tubes at high heat flux), **stainless-steel tubes for the nozzle** (lower heat flux there,
tubes give adequate cooling at lower weight) `[p.42, p.49]`.

**Real chamber-pressure cooling limits by propellant/coolant** (staged-combustion power
cycle, coolant inlet-to-chamber-pressure ratio Pin/Pc = 1.8 or 2.25, 250-cycle x 4 safety-
factor service life) `[Aerospike-CR135231 p.37-38, p.52-53]`:
- O2/RP-1 or O2/RJ-5 cooled **with RP-1/RJ-5 fuel itself**: limited to Pc ≈ 0.372×10⁷ N/m²
  (540 psia) / 0.483×10⁷ N/m² (700 psia) at Pin/Pc=1.8/2.25 respectively, capped by a
  **316 °C (600 °F) coolant-side wall temperature coking limit** — RP-1/RJ-5 coking starts
  ~263 °C (500 °F) and accelerates rapidly above it; severity is much lower below ~368 °C
  (700 °F) at high coolant velocity.
- O2/RP-1 combustor cooled **with oxygen** instead: raises the limit to Pc ≈ 1.378×10⁷ N/m²
  (2000 psia) / 1.722×10⁷ N/m² (2500 psia) — gas-side wall temps 492–551 °C (918–1024 °F).
- O2/RP-1 combustor cooled **with hydrogen** (borrowed from the inner LOX/LH2 combustor's
  supply): gives the *highest* achievable chamber pressure of the three coolants for
  O2/RP-1, but at the cost of reduced H2 flow available for the inner combustor and nozzle
  — the nozzle cooling limit correspondingly drops to Pc ≈ 1.722×10⁷ N/m² (2500 psia) when
  H2 is shared this way.
- O2/CH4 is **slightly harder to cool** than O2/RP-1 (higher heat flux): Pc limits of
  1.24×10⁷ N/m² (1800 psia) / 1.586×10⑦ N/m² (2300 psia) at the same Pin/Pc ratios.
- Oxygen coolant carries a stated hazard: a gas-side-wall O2 leak mixing to a
  near-stoichiometric ratio with the mainstream combustion gas right at the wall can cause
  local wall overheating — flagged as a real risk, not just a heat-transfer-correlation
  caveat `[p.42-43]`.

**Width-to-height (W/H) ratio directly trades against coolability** `[Aerospike-CR135231
p.65, "Width-to-Height Ratio Influence," Table 9]`: for fixed thrust and chamber pressure,
increasing engine W/H ratio increases hot-gas-wetted surface area while available coolant
flow stays fixed — both required coolant flow and coolant ΔP rise substantially at higher
W/H for the same cyclic-life target. This is a linear-aerospike-specific geometric coupling
(a slot engine's aspect ratio is a free design variable a round bell doesn't have) worth
flagging as a design-driver if `engine_designer/` ever gains a linear/slot-throat mode.

### Structural design specific to the linear-spike geometry

`[Aerospike-CR135231 p.169-172, "Structure"]` — real Task VI preliminary-design hardware
description, ~20×10⁶ N (4.5×10⁶ lbf) thrust-class point design:
- **Modular truss frame**: 14 tubular trusses per module (baseline material **heat-treated
  AISI 4340 low-alloy steel**), joined to the opposing module's corresponding truss to form
  continuous trusses spanning mounting-pad to mounting-pad. 7 bolted intermodule joints +
  14 crossover struts (chosen over 14 bolted joints specifically to leave more open space
  for the large turbopumps). The 14-truss count itself derives from a combustor
  *segment width* of ~30 cm (12 in) found to be the minimum-weight segment size from past
  analysis — segment-width optimization is explicitly a structural (not thermal/performance)
  driver.
- **Combustor liner structural problem, unique to the flat/slot chamber**: each combustor
  liner's **flat sides** (the two long walls of the linear throat slot) are exposed to high
  internal pressure and require external stiffening beams to prevent deflection — i.e.
  *opening of the throat gap under pressure*, a failure mode with no analog in an
  axisymmetric chamber (which reacts hoop stress symmetrically; a flat/slot wall instead
  bends). Beam weight rises rapidly with segment width, reinforcing the ~30 cm (12 in)
  optimum segment width above from the structural side too `[p.179-180]`.
- **Combustor shroud divergence**: downstream of the throat gap, each combustor's shroud
  diverges in **two dimensions** (not just one, as a bell/conical nozzle's wall would) so
  that the exiting jet sheets fill the full width of the shared spike surface without gaps
  between adjacent module segments `[p.180]`.
- **Nozzle + "fences" construction**: the spike nozzle itself and two lateral **fences**
  (edge walls closing off the sides of the linear spike, preventing lateral spillage of the
  expanding flow) are built as parallel continuous coolant tubes welded together into an
  impervious panel — nozzle supported by hat-section beams + pivoted struts; fences
  cantilevered off the ends of the nozzle beams via their own hat-section beams `[p.180]`.
  A portion of the inner combustor's high-pressure-pump hydrogen flow cools the nozzle and
  fences before being routed to the turbine preburner (this coolant flow is *reused*, not a
  separate dedicated supply).
- **Coolant manifolds encircle each combustor** (both inlet and outlet, for both inner and
  outer combustors) and consume physical space between adjacent combustor segments — this
  inter-segment spacing requirement (for both structure and manifolding) sets a lower bound
  on combustor width alongside the ~30 cm structural optimum above `[p.180]`.

### Thrust vector control — a real linear-aerospike-specific mechanism

`[Aerospike-CR135231 p.196-197, "Thrust Vector Control," Table 30, Fig. 129-130]`: since a
fixed linear-aerospike engine has **no gimbal** (the whole point of a modular clustered
engine sharing one spike is that gimbaling any part of it is impractical), TVC is achieved
purely by **differential throttling** between modules on opposite sides of the vehicle
centerline. The report gives closed-form equivalent-gimbal-angle formulas:

- Yaw: θ_y = sin⁻¹[ W·X·(1−X)/(2·L_g) · (%T/100) ]
- Pitch: θ_p = sin⁻¹[ H·X·(1−X)/L_g · (%T/100) ]
- Net thrust: F_net = F − X·F·(%T/100)

where W = engine width, H = nozzle height, L_g = length from vehicle/engine interface to
vehicle C.G., X = fraction of modules throttled, %T = percent throttling depth of the
throttled modules. Fig. 130 shows the resulting equivalent gimbal angle vs. throttling
percent for a family of L_g/H and L_g/W ratios — at realistic geometry the achievable
equivalent gimbal angles are small (single digits of degrees) for throttling depths up to
~50%, i.e. **linear-engine differential-throttling TVC is a materially weaker control
authority than a gimbaled bell engine**, a genuine trade-off of the concept.

### Real weight and point-design numbers

`[Aerospike-CR135231 Table 29 p.170, Table 31 p.201]` — two Task VI point designs at
17.8×10⁶ N (4×10⁶ lbf) sea-level / 20.0×10⁶ N (4.5×10⁶ lbf) vacuum Mode-1 thrust,
7.36×10⁶ N (1.65×10⁶ lbf) vacuum Mode-2 thrust, thrust split 0.65, Mode-1 ε=40, Mode-2
ε=114, engine width-to-nozzle-height ratio 3.0:
- **Concept 1** (O2/RP-1 outer / gas-generator cycle + O2/H2 inner / staged-combustion
  cycle): Pc,outer=1380 N/cm² (2000 psia), Pc,inner=1724 N/cm² (2500 psia); envelope
  8.8×4.4×2.1 m (346×172×84.5 in); **total engine weight 20,070 kg (44,250 lbm)**.
- **Concept 2** (both combustors O2/H2, both staged-combustion): same chamber pressures and
  envelope; **total engine weight 22,850 kg (50,380 lbm)** — 22% heavier than Concept 1,
  attributed to the outer combustor's higher operating pressure and different propellant
  combination when both stages are hydrolox.
- Weight breakdown by major component (turbopump+mount, ducting, valves, hot-gas system,
  combustion chamber+shroud, nozzle+support, base closure, ignition, controls) is tabulated
  separately for outer/inner engine of each concept (Table 31) — useful as a rough
  fraction-of-total-weight reference for a linear/modular architecture, though absolute
  numbers are specific to this ~4.5×10⁶ lbf thrust class and this era's (SSME-level)
  materials technology, explicitly stated as the assumed technology baseline `[p.204]`.

### Concept-level takeaways

- The dual-fuel split-combustor concept, Mode-1/Mode-2 operation, and 4-module baseline are
  all specific to this study's SSTO mission requirement (mixed-mode propellant-density
  optimization) — **not** generic requirements of a linear aerospike; a single-propellant
  linear aerospike is a materially simpler concept than everything this report studies.
- Module count trade (p.20-28): 4 modules recommended as the sweet spot minimizing engine
  weight and maximizing performance across the study's thrust-split range; more modules
  (up to 10 evaluated) cost ~9 N·s/kg (1 sec) of performance from 2→10 modules due to
  shrinking, less-efficient individual turbopumps, despite finer throttling granularity.
- Hydrogen is identified as the best all-around coolant for every combustor/nozzle
  combination studied but the engine architecture is explicitly stated to tolerate
  supplemental cooling by the second fuel or by oxygen if mission trades favor higher
  chamber pressure over coolant-flow economy `[p.204, Conclusions]`.

## Design method

This is a parametric point-design study, not a general design-methodology source: its
"method" is largely mission/thrust-split/area-ratio trade-space generation (Tasks I, IV, V)
feeding one specific contour-generation procedure (MOC spike + truncated-shroud matching,
Task III) and one specific structural layout (Task VI). None of it is reducible to a
general closed-form nozzle-sizing equation the way Sutton/Huzel are for bells — treat it as
**real precedent data for one linear-aerospike architecture**, not a general aerospike
design-equation source.

## Caveats

- **`engine_designer/` currently has NO aerospike/plug-nozzle physics at all** — only
  conical and Rao-bell nozzle shapes are modeled (`physics/nozzle_shapes.py`). Everything
  extracted above is reference material for a hypothetical future linear-aerospike or
  plug-nozzle feature; it does not fill any existing gap in the tool's current physics, and
  none of these numbers/methods should be wired into any existing bell/conical code path.
- This report studies one very specific (dual-fuel, split-combustor, modular, SSTO-sized)
  linear aerospike architecture at ~4.5×10⁶ lbf thrust class — orders of magnitude larger
  than any engine currently modeled in this project's `Engine_Configs/` catalog or designed
  in `engine_designer/`. Scaling any of these numbers (weights, structural dimensions,
  cooling limits) down to KSP-RO-scale engines (typically 10³–10⁶ N) would need real
  scaling-law justification, not direct reuse.
- **No base-region flow physics** (base pressure, base bleed, base heating, recirculation)
  is given anywhere in the extracted material — the "base closure" is treated purely as a
  structural/geometric hardware feature, not a flow phenomenon the report analyzes. This is
  a real gap: a full aerospike altitude-compensation/performance model would need base-flow
  physics this report doesn't supply, and would have to be sourced elsewhere (e.g. classic
  aerospike literature — Rao's original plug-nozzle papers, or later X-33/Linear Aerospike
  program reports — neither in `literature/` currently).
- **No quantitative altitude-compensation curve** (e.g. thrust or Isp vs. altitude showing
  the aerospike's efficiency advantage over a fixed bell) is given — the "automatic
  continuous altitude compensation" property is stated once as a known fact of the
  aerospike class, not derived or plotted anywhere in the extracted sections.
- 1976–77 vintage, explicitly designed against **Space Shuttle Main Engine (SSME)-era**
  materials technology (stated directly, p.204) — predates any composite/modern-alloy
  aerospike work (e.g. X-33 XRS-2200, 1990s) and the report itself flags composite
  materials investigation as unstarted future work (Table 32).
- OCR quality is poor throughout (see Character section) — all quotations/paraphrases above
  are reconstructed, treat exact wording as paraphrase-grade, not verbatim.
- Large fractions of the report (Tasks I, IV, V — roughly 100 of 236 pages) are pure
  parametric chart/table data not deep-read in this pass; if a future need arises for a
  *specific* point on one of those trade curves (e.g. exact Isp at a given area ratio/
  chamber pressure/thrust split combination for one of the four propellant pairs), it can
  be read directly off the relevant figure (e.g. Fig. 4-12 for theoretical vacuum/sea-level
  Isp) rather than re-extracted narratively.
